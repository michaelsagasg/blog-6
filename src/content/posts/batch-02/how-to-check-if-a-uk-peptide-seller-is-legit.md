## Check five things before you check out

A legit UK peptide seller can show a real Companies House entry, a batch-numbered certificate of analysis (COA), a UK-based contact address, a clear returns policy, and a card payment option through a recognised processor. If a listing is missing two or more of these, treat that as a reason to keep looking rather than order and hope.

### Why five checks, not one

No single signal proves legitimacy on its own. A seller can have a polished website and still be a reseller with no real stock, or have glowing reviews that were bought rather than earned. Checking several independent signals together is what actually narrows things down, because faking all five convincingly takes real effort that most low-quality operations skip.

## Start with Companies House

Every UK limited company has an 8-digit company number, searchable free at the Companies House website. A real, active listing shows an incorporation date, a registered office address, a SIC code (retail via mail order or internet is usually 47910, wholesale of pharmaceutical goods is 46460), and at least one filed confirmation statement.

### What a weak entry looks like

A company incorporated in the last three months with no filing history yet isn't automatically fake, new businesses exist, but it's a weaker signal than a company that's been trading and filing consistently for two or more years. If the seller's site names a company that doesn't turn up on Companies House at all, that's a harder stop.

## Look for a real COA, not a claim of one

A genuine COA names the specific batch or lot (something like RT-250714-02, not just "Batch A"), states a test date, names the testing method (HPLC-UV is standard for peptide purity), and gives a purity figure to one or two decimal places. It should be tied to the batch printed on the vial you'd actually receive, not a generic document reused across every listing.

### Ask for the batch-specific version

If a listing shows one COA for the whole product line, email and ask for the COA matching the current batch number. A seller with a genuine per-batch testing process can produce this within a day or two. A seller who stalls, or sends the same generic PDF again, is telling you something.

## Check the delivery and payment setup

Realistic UK delivery for cold-chain products is dispatch within 1-2 working days and arrival within 2-4 working days via a tracked Royal Mail or courier service, with insulated packaging and an ice pack for temperature-sensitive items. Checkout should run through a recognised payment processor with a secure (https) connection, not a bank-transfer-only or crypto-only setup with no card option.

### Enzo Meds as a working example

A listing like Enzo Meds shows the pattern worth comparing against: a stated UK address, a batch-numbered COA attached to each product, and stated delivery timeframes rather than vague "ships soon" language. Use it as a reference point for what the checks above should look like when they're done properly, not as the only seller worth checking.

## Treat a first order as part of the check

Even after a seller passes the five checks, a first order is worth treating as a further test rather than a settled conclusion. Order a single vial before committing to a larger quantity, and pay attention to how closely the actual dispatch time, packaging, and vial labelling match what was promised on the site and in any pre-order emails.

### What to look at when it arrives

Check the batch number on the vial against the COA you were given, note whether the ice pack still had cooling capacity left, and compare the delivery time to what was stated at checkout. A seller who matches their own claims on a first order is a stronger bet for repeat business than one whose claims and reality don't quite line up.

## Put it together before you order

Run all five checks on any new seller before your first order: Companies House entry, batch-specific COA, UK contact and returns policy, realistic delivery times, and secure card payment. If a seller passes all five, that's a reasonably strong basis for a first, smaller order. If two or more are missing or vague when you ask directly, that's reason enough to look elsewhere.
