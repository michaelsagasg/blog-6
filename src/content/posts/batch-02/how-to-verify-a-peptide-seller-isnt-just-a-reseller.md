## Why this distinction matters

A seller reselling another supplier's stock without doing or commissioning its own batch testing is adding a layer of risk without adding a layer of verification. The product you receive is only as reliable as whatever testing happened further up the chain, which you likely can't see or check directly. Knowing whether a seller tests independently, or simply trusts and repackages someone else's claims, changes how much weight to put on their COA.

### Reselling isn't inherently dishonest

Plenty of legitimate UK sellers don't manufacture their own peptides; almost none do. The distinction that matters is whether the seller does or commissions its own batch-specific testing on the stock it actually holds, rather than simply passing along a manufacturer's or upstream supplier's claims unchecked.

## Ask whether the COA is theirs or a manufacturer's

Check whether the COA attached to a listing states the seller's own commissioned testing (naming a lab the seller worked with directly) or is clearly a manufacturer's generic certificate with no seller-specific batch link. A seller who commissions their own testing on the specific batch they hold is doing meaningfully more verification work than one who just forwards whatever document came with the stock.

### A question worth emailing

Ask directly: "Do you test each batch yourselves, or rely on the manufacturer's documentation?" A seller confident in their own process usually answers this clearly and specifically. A vague or evasive answer suggests they may not know the answer themselves, which is its own kind of red flag.

## Look for consistency across their catalogue

A seller genuinely testing their own stock usually shows a consistent COA format across their whole catalogue, same lab, same layout, since it's their own established process. A seller whose COAs look completely different from product to product, different fonts, different lab names, different formats, is more likely stitching together documents from multiple upstream sources without a consistent testing relationship of their own.

### Check how batch numbers are structured

A seller with their own internal testing and batch tracking usually uses a consistent internal batch numbering format across products (something like a prefix plus date plus sequence). Batch numbers that look completely different in structure from product to product can indicate stock sourced from multiple unrelated suppliers with no unified process behind it.

## Consider what a pure reseller can't tell you

A pure reseller generally can't answer detailed questions about storage conditions before the stock reached them, how long it sat in transit, or specifics about the manufacturing process, since they weren't involved in any of it. A seller with more direct involvement in sourcing and testing can usually speak to these details with more confidence and specificity.

### Independent testing as the strongest check

Regardless of whether a seller tests in-house or relies on upstream documentation, sending a sample to your own independently chosen lab remains the most direct way to verify what's actually in the vial, particularly for a first or larger order from a seller you're still getting to know.

## What this looks like done well

A seller such as Enzo Meds attaches a batch-numbered COA consistent in format across its listings and can speak specifically to its sourcing and testing process, which is a stronger position than a seller simply forwarding whatever documentation happened to arrive with the stock.

## Watch for pricing that suggests a long supply chain

A seller several links removed from the original manufacturing, buying from another reseller who bought from another reseller, generally has to price higher to cover each markup, or cut corners on testing and packaging to stay competitive. Pricing that seems inconsistent with either a short, direct supply chain or genuine independent testing is worth factoring into this assessment.

### Ask how long they've worked with their supplier

A seller with a stable, long-standing relationship with a specific manufacturer or supplier can usually describe it, even in general terms, more confidently than one sourcing opportunistically from whoever has stock available. This isn't something you can verify directly, but the confidence and consistency of the answer over time is informative.

## In short

Ask directly whether a seller tests their own stock or relies on upstream documentation, check for consistency in COA format and batch numbering across the catalogue, and consider independent testing for a first or higher-value order. A pure reseller isn't automatically untrustworthy, but it's a meaningfully weaker verification position than a seller with their own testing process.
