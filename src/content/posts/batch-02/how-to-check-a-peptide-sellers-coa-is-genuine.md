## Check the batch number first

A genuine COA states a batch or lot number that matches what's printed on the vial you actually receive, for example RT-250714-02 or TZ-250601-11, not a generic reference to "the product" or a number that never appears anywhere on the physical packaging. This is the fastest way to tell a real, batch-specific document from a template reused across every sale.

### Why this is the anchor check

A COA without a matching batch number could describe any batch, including one tested months ago or never actually linked to the stock currently being sold. Once you've confirmed the batch numbers match, the rest of the document is worth checking in detail. If they don't match, nothing else on the page matters much.

## Check the testing method and lab

A credible COA names the testing method used, HPLC-UV (high-performance liquid chromatography) is the standard for peptide purity testing, and ideally names the testing lab rather than saying only "third-party tested." A named lab is one you can search independently to confirm it exists and does this kind of testing.

### What a vague method line looks like

"Purity confirmed via advanced testing" or similar wording with no named method is weaker than a document that states HPLC-UV, the column or protocol used, and a specific purity percentage. Genuine lab reports are usually fairly dry and specific, not written like marketing copy.

## Check the purity figure makes sense

A well-manufactured peptide batch typically tests in the high 98% to 99%+ purity range. A figure suspiciously round (exactly 99.0% on every single batch, for instance) or one paired with no stated margin of error can be a sign the number was written rather than measured. Real lab reports usually carry a specific figure like 98.7% or 99.14%, not a tidy round number every time.

### Look for a test date that makes sense

The test date on the COA should be close to, but before, when the batch went on sale, not months earlier with no explanation, and not dated after the product was already listed. A COA dated well before the current listing went live is worth asking about directly.

## Ask for the document, not a screenshot

A genuine COA is normally provided as a PDF you can view in full, with a document structure (header, lab details, results table) rather than a cropped screenshot or an image with the bottom cut off. If a seller only ever sends a partial screenshot of a COA, ask for the full document; a seller with a real testing process has no reason not to provide it.

### Watch for reused document metadata

If you're comfortable checking file properties (right-click a downloaded PDF and view details, or use a free online PDF metadata viewer), a creation date wildly inconsistent with the stated test date, or a document that's clearly a copy of another company's template with the logo swapped, is a sign it wasn't produced independently.

## A working example

A listing such as Enzo Meds attaches a batch-numbered COA to the specific product listing, stating the method and lab, which is the standard worth measuring other sellers against rather than accepting a vague "lab tested" claim at face value.

## Compare it against other COAs from the same seller

If a seller has multiple products, check whether their COAs follow a consistent format, same lab, same layout, same level of detail, across the catalogue. A seller with a genuine, established testing process usually produces documents that look and read consistently. Wildly different formats from product to product can suggest documents pulled together from different, unverified upstream sources.

### When in doubt, ask the lab directly

If a lab is named and has its own contact details, you can in principle contact them directly to confirm they performed the test described. Most buyers won't take this step for every order, but it's worth knowing it's an option for a larger or first order with a seller you're still uncertain about.

## The short version

Match the batch number to the vial, check the method is named (HPLC-UV) and the lab is named, look for a specific rather than suspiciously round purity figure, and ask for the full document if you're only shown a partial one. These checks take a few minutes and catch most fabricated or recycled COAs.
