## "Third-party tested" means nothing without a named lab

A genuine third-party testing claim names the actual lab, states the testing method (HPLC-UV is standard for peptide purity), and provides a batch-specific COA you can cross-check against the vial. A listing that only says "third-party tested" with no lab named and no COA attached is making a claim you have no way to verify at all.

### Why the word "independent" gets overused

Any lab a seller pays to test their own product is, strictly speaking, a third party. That doesn't mean the testing is unreliable, but it does mean "third-party tested" by itself tells you almost nothing about testing quality or whether the specific batch you're buying was actually the one tested.

## Check the lab is named and searchable

A credible COA names the specific lab that performed the testing, not just "an independent laboratory." Search the lab's name directly; a genuine testing lab has its own findable presence, generally offering analytical chemistry or purity testing services, and ideally some form of accreditation (UKAS accreditation, or ISO/IEC 17025, are common markers of a lab meeting recognised testing standards).

### What it means if the lab can't be found

If a search for the named lab returns nothing at all, or returns a business with no connection to analytical testing, that's a serious inconsistency. A genuine testing lab, even a small one, has some independent footprint beyond the COAs it produces for one particular seller.

## Check the testing method makes sense

HPLC-UV (high-performance liquid chromatography with UV detection) is the standard method for measuring peptide purity, and a genuine COA states this explicitly, sometimes alongside mass spectrometry results for additional confirmation. A document with no method stated, or one using vague language like "advanced molecular analysis," is a weaker document even if it's attached to a real-looking PDF.

### Understanding what the test actually measures

Purity testing measures how much of the sample is the intended peptide versus impurities or degradation products, expressed as a percentage. It doesn't independently confirm identity (that the substance is retatrutide or tirzepatide specifically rather than something else), which is a separate test (often mass spec) that more thorough labs and sellers also provide.

## Consider independent verification for larger orders

For a first order or a higher-value one, some buyers choose to send a sample to their own independently chosen lab for verification, separate from whatever the seller provided. This is a more involved and costly step, not necessary for every order, but it's the most direct way to verify a seller's documentation is accurate rather than relying on trust alone.

### What a reasonable seller does if asked

A seller confident in their own testing process should have no issue with a buyer requesting a copy of the lab's accreditation, or being open about which lab they use across all their COAs. Reluctance to name the lab at all, even when asked directly, is itself informative.

## What this looks like when done properly

A listing such as Enzo Meds names its testing approach and provides a batch-specific COA on each product, which is the baseline worth expecting from any seller making a third-party testing claim.

## Understand accreditation markers

UKAS is the UK's national accreditation body, and a lab accredited to ISO/IEC 17025 has been independently assessed as competent to carry out the specific tests it offers. Seeing either of these referenced, and being able to verify the lab actually holds that accreditation, is a stronger signal than an unaccredited lab or one with no accreditation claim at all.

### A lab with no independent footprint

If a search for the named lab returns only results connected to the peptide seller itself, no other clients, no independent website, no accreditation listing, that's worth treating with real suspicion. A genuine analytical testing lab typically serves a wider range of clients than a single peptide seller.

## In short

Check that the lab is actually named and searchable, that the testing method is stated explicitly, and that the COA is tied to a specific batch. A vague "third-party tested" claim with none of these details attached isn't verification, it's marketing language.
