## Verify the company, the COA, and the price

Before ordering tirzepatide from a UK seller, check three things directly: a real Companies House entry, a batch-specific COA attached to the exact product you're buying, and a price that fits the range genuine sellers actually charge. Tirzepatide is one of the most searched peptides in this market, which means it's also one of the most imitated.

### Why demand attracts weaker sellers

Because tirzepatide gets so much search volume, it's the product category where new, unverified sellers show up fastest, often reselling stock they haven't tested themselves. That doesn't make every new seller untrustworthy, but it means the verification steps below are worth doing every time, not just for unfamiliar names.

## Confirm the company on Companies House

Every genuine UK limited company has an 8-digit company number you can look up free on the Companies House website. Check the incorporation date, the registered office address, the SIC code (mail order or internet retail is typically 47910), and whether accounts or a confirmation statement have actually been filed. A company with no filing history and a registration date a few weeks old is a weaker signal, not an automatic disqualifier.

### Match the trading name to the legal name

Sellers sometimes trade under a different name than their registered company. That's legal and common, but the site should state the registered company name somewhere (usually the footer or terms page) so you can actually run the check. If you can't find a company name to search at all, that's a bigger concern than a young company.

## Check the COA matches the batch

A genuine tirzepatide COA states the specific batch or lot number (something like TZ-250601-11), a test date, the testing method (HPLC-UV is standard), and a purity figure, usually in the 98-99%+ range for a well-manufactured batch from a competent lab. That batch number needs to match what's printed on the vial you actually receive, not just describe the product line in general.

### If the COA looks generic

A single COA reused across every batch and every restock is a warning sign. Email and ask for the COA tied to current stock before ordering. A response within a working day or two, with a document that actually names a batch, method, and date, is what a real per-batch testing process looks like.

## Compare the price to the market

Tirzepatide typically runs cheaper per mg than retatrutide. As of 2026, a 5mg vial commonly sits around £70-£110 and a 10mg vial around £120-£180 from established UK sellers. Pricing significantly below that range, especially combined with no COA or a generic one, is one of the clearer signals that something is off with the batch.

A listing like Enzo Meds, showing a UK address, a batch-numbered COA on the tirzepatide listing, and stated delivery times, is a useful benchmark for what a verified listing should look like, alongside any other seller you're comparing it to.

## Watch for consistency across the whole catalogue

Once you've checked one tirzepatide listing carefully, spot-check a couple of others from the same seller. A seller with a genuine per-batch testing process usually shows the same level of documentation, format, and specificity across their whole catalogue, not just their best-selling product. Noticeably weaker documentation on other listings is worth factoring into your overall impression of the seller.

### Check how stock levels are handled

A seller genuinely tracking real batch-specific inventory will show a product as out of stock between batches from time to time. A catalogue where nothing is ever out of stock, combined with a batch number that never changes, suggests inventory isn't being tracked as closely as the listing implies.

## Before you order

Look up the company, request a batch-matching COA if one isn't already visible, and check the price against the ranges above. These three checks take about twenty minutes on a new site and catch the large majority of sellers worth avoiding before you've spent any money.
