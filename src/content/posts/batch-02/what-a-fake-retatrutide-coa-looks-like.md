## The tell-tale signs of a fake COA

A fake or unreliable retatrutide COA typically has no batch number, or one that doesn't match the vial you receive, a vague or missing testing method, no named testing lab, a suspiciously round purity figure repeated across every batch, and a document format that looks copied or edited from a template. Any one of these is worth noticing; several together mean the document isn't proving what it claims to.

### Why this matters more for retatrutide

Retatrutide is a newer, more complex peptide than tirzepatide or semaglutide, and genuine testing for it is more involved and more expensive. That combination, high demand and higher testing costs, is exactly what makes a fabricated or reused COA an attractive shortcut for a low-quality seller trying to look legitimate cheaply.

## No batch number, or one that doesn't match

A genuine COA states a specific batch or lot number (something like RT-250714-02) that matches what's printed on the vial you receive. A COA with no batch number at all, or one describing "the product" generically, can't be tied to the specific stock being shipped to you, which makes it functionally meaningless as verification.

### The same COA across multiple listings

If you find the identical COA image, same batch number, same test date, attached to listings from more than one unrelated seller, that's strong evidence it's been copied rather than produced from genuine testing of that seller's own stock.

## Vague or missing testing method

A genuine COA names the method used, HPLC-UV is the standard for peptide purity. A document that only says "advanced testing" or "third-party verified" with no method named, and no lab named, gives you nothing to check independently.

### A purity figure that's too clean

A well-manufactured batch typically tests somewhere in the high 98% to 99%+ range, with a specific, non-round figure like 98.7% or 99.14%. A COA showing exactly 99.0% or 99.9% on every single batch, month after month, is a pattern more consistent with a number that was written rather than measured.

## Document formatting inconsistencies

Watch for a document that looks like a template with the logo swapped, mismatched fonts within the same document, a lab letterhead that doesn't match the stated lab name elsewhere in the text, or a creation date in the file's own metadata that doesn't match the stated test date. These are harder to fake convincingly and worth checking if you're able to view the PDF's file properties.

### A test date that doesn't line up

The test date should sit shortly before the batch went on sale, not months earlier with no explanation, and not after the listing already went live. A COA dated well out of step with the stated batch or listing timeline is worth asking about directly.

## What a genuine COA looks like by comparison

A listing such as Enzo Meds shows a COA with a specific, current batch number, a named method and lab, and a purity figure consistent with a real test result, matching the batch printed on the vial itself. Comparing any other seller's retatrutide COA against this pattern is a reasonable way to spot the gaps.

## Cross-check against other sellers' documents

If you're comparing several retatrutide listings, keep the COAs open side by side. A genuinely independent lab's report has its own consistent formatting across different clients; documents that are identical in layout but attributed to different labs, or that share unusual formatting quirks despite claiming different sources, suggest a common, likely fabricated, origin.

### When a seller pushes back on scrutiny

A seller who reacts defensively, or refuses outright, when you ask reasonable questions about their COA, the lab's accreditation, the test date, is behaving differently from one confident in their own documentation. This reaction itself is informative, regardless of whether the document turns out to be genuine or not.

## In short

Check for a matching batch number, a named method and lab, a plausible (not suspiciously round) purity figure, and a test date consistent with the listing. A COA missing several of these, or one that turns up identically on other sellers' listings, is not reliable evidence of anything.
