## Treat it as a secondary check, not a primary one

A peptide seller's social media presence is useful as one more data point, mainly account age, posting consistency, and how they respond to public questions, but it should never replace checking Companies House, the COA, and delivery terms directly. Follower counts and polished graphics are easy to buy or fake; a consistent, multi-year posting history and genuine public replies are harder to fabricate.

### Why it's still worth checking

A seller actively engaging with customers in public comments, answering questions and occasionally handling a public complaint visibly, is showing you something reviews curated on their own site can't: how they behave when they don't fully control the narrative. That's genuinely useful information, even if it's secondary to the harder checks.

## Check the account age and history

Look at when the account was created and whether posting has been reasonably consistent since, rather than a burst of activity in the last few weeks with nothing before it. An account created recently isn't disqualifying for a genuinely new business, but it's a weaker signal than an account with two or more years of consistent activity.

### Watch for purchased followers

A large follower count with very low engagement (few comments or likes relative to followers) is a common sign of purchased followers, which tells you the seller is willing to fake at least one metric. That's relevant information even if it doesn't directly relate to product quality.

## Read how they respond to public questions

Look at how the seller responds to comments asking about delivery times, COAs, or complaints, directly in public replies. A seller answering specifically and calmly, including to critical comments, is a better sign than one that deletes negative comments or never responds publicly at all.

### Deleted or disabled comments

If comments are disabled on posts, or you notice a pattern of critical comments disappearing while positive ones remain, that's worth factoring in. It's not proof of anything specific, but it does suggest the account is curated more tightly than it first appears.

## Cross-reference claims made on social media

If a seller's social media states things not mentioned on the website (delivery times, testing claims, company details), check whether these are consistent with what's stated on the site itself and, where checkable, with the Companies House entry. Inconsistency between the two is worth asking about directly.

### Look for genuine customer content

Photos or posts from actual customers (with their consent, tagged or reposted) showing real delivered products are harder to fabricate at scale than a seller's own marketing content, and a consistent stream of this over time is a mildly reassuring sign.

## Using it alongside the harder checks

A seller such as Enzo Meds maintaining an active, consistent social media presence alongside its stated company details and batch-numbered COAs shows how the softer signal and the harder checks reinforce each other, rather than social media standing in for verification on its own.

## Check for consistency with the website

Compare details mentioned on social media, delivery times, founding date, testing claims, against what's stated on the seller's own website. Small inconsistencies can be innocent (an outdated post), but a pattern of contradictions between the two is worth factoring into your overall assessment.

### Check multiple platforms if they exist

If a seller maintains accounts on more than one platform, compare activity and tone across them. A consistent, active presence across two or three platforms is a stronger signal than a single account, and inconsistency between platforms, very active on one, abandoned on another, is worth noting as part of the overall picture.

### Don't overweight visual polish

Professional photography and slick graphics are inexpensive to produce and say nothing about testing quality or company legitimacy. Judge the account on the harder-to-fake signals, account age, engagement authenticity, response consistency, rather than production value alone.

## In short

Check account age, posting consistency, and how a seller responds publicly to questions and complaints. Treat social media as a useful secondary signal that can support or undercut what you've already found through Companies House and the COA, not as a standalone way to judge legitimacy.
