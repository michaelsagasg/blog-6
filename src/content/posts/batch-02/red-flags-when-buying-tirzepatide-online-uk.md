## The five clearest red flags

When buying tirzepatide online in the UK, the clearest warning signs are: no batch-specific COA, no findable Companies House entry, a price well under the typical £120-£180 range for a 10mg vial, no working UK contact route, and a checkout with no card payment option. Because tirzepatide is one of the most searched peptides in this market, it's also one of the most commonly imitated, so these checks are worth doing on every new seller.

### Why the search volume matters here

High demand means new, unverified sites appear constantly trying to capture tirzepatide searches, some reselling stock they've never tested themselves. That doesn't mean every new seller is untrustworthy, but it means you can't rely on a site simply existing and ranking well as a sign of legitimacy.

## No batch-specific COA

A genuine COA names the exact batch or lot (for example TZ-250601-11), states a test date, names the testing method (HPLC-UV is standard for peptide purity), and gives a purity figure, typically 98% or higher for a well-manufactured batch. A listing with no COA, or the same generic document attached to every restock regardless of batch, doesn't actually verify what's in your vial.

### "Available on request" is a soft red flag

If a COA is only offered after you ask, rather than shown on the listing, that's not disqualifying by itself, but it shifts the burden of verification onto you before you've paid anything. A seller confident in their testing process usually just shows it.

## No Companies House entry

Search the company name stated on the site (check the footer or terms page if it's not obvious) on Companies House. A real entry has an 8-digit company number, an incorporation date, a registered office, and at least a filed confirmation statement. If the name doesn't return anything, or returns a dissolved company, treat that as a serious concern rather than a minor gap.

### Trading name versus legal name

Many legitimate sellers trade under a brand name different from their registered company name, which is normal. The problem is when no legal company name is stated anywhere on the site, since that removes your ability to check anything at all.

## Pricing that's unrealistically low

Tirzepatide from established UK sellers commonly runs £70-£110 for a 5mg vial and £120-£180 for a 10mg vial as of 2026. A site offering tirzepatide significantly below that, particularly a new site with no COA and no reviews, is one of the more reliable signals that the material is underdosed, mislabelled, or not what it claims to be.

### Bundle deals worth a second look

"Buy 3, get 2 free" style bundles on a peptide this specialised are worth more scrutiny than the same offer on a low-cost consumer product, since the margin required to genuinely test and source at that discount is thin.

## No card payment, no real contact details

A checkout that only accepts bank transfer or cryptocurrency, with no card processor involved, skips the fraud and merchant-vetting checks that card processors normally apply to a business. Combined with no visible UK contact address or a contact form that never gets a response, this is one of the stronger red flag combinations.

### A working example of the alternative

A listing like Enzo Meds, with a UK address, a batch-numbered COA on the tirzepatide listing, and standard card checkout, is a useful comparison for what a listing without these red flags actually looks like.

## Marketing language standing in for documentation

Phrases like "pharma grade" or "highest purity guaranteed" with no batch number, method, or lab named behind them are marketing claims, not verification. A seller leaning on this kind of language instead of a specific COA is asking you to take their word for something a real document would otherwise prove.

### Generic product photography

A listing showing only stock imagery, with no genuine photo of the actual vial or box, leaves you with nothing to check against the batch number on any COA that is provided. Combined with vague sourcing language, this is a pattern worth treating as a real warning sign rather than a minor style choice.

## Putting it together

A young company or a modest discount isn't a reason to walk away on its own. But a tirzepatide listing with no COA, no Companies House entry, and pricing well below market rate all at once is worth avoiding, and even two of these together are reason enough to ask direct questions before you order.
