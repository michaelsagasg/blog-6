## Check the listing details, not just the product photo

A fake or misleading tirzepatide listing usually shows itself through mismatched details: a product photo that doesn't match the stated mg strength, no batch number anywhere, a COA that's generic or missing, and pricing well below what genuine sourcing and testing actually costs. Checking these details takes a few minutes and catches most low-quality listings before you order.

### Why tirzepatide specifically gets faked often

Tirzepatide is one of the highest-demand peptides searched in the UK, which means it's also one of the most commonly relabelled or diluted by low-quality operations trying to capture that search volume. High demand doesn't cause the fakes, but it makes tirzepatide listings worth extra scrutiny compared to less-searched products.

## Check the vial labelling described or photographed

A genuine vial label states the peptide name, the mg strength, a batch or lot number, and usually a manufacture or expiry reference. A listing showing only a generic stock photo, or a label with no batch number visible at all, gives you nothing to cross-check against the COA even if one is provided.

### Mismatched strength and photo

If the listing sells a 10mg vial but the product photo clearly shows a label reading 5mg, or the photo is reused across multiple different strength listings with only the price changed, that inconsistency is worth treating as a real warning sign rather than a formatting oversight.

## Check for a batch-specific COA

A genuine COA names the batch (something like TZ-250601-11), the test date, the method (HPLC-UV is standard), and a purity figure, usually 98% or above for a well-manufactured batch. A listing with no COA, a COA image too low-resolution to actually read, or one clearly copied from another company's template with the logo changed, is not verifying anything.

### The reused-COA test

If you can find the same COA image (via a reverse image search, or by comparing it to COAs shown by other unrelated sellers) attached to multiple different sellers' listings, that's strong evidence the document isn't specific to that seller's actual stock.

## Check the price against the market

Tirzepatide from established UK sellers commonly runs £70-£110 for a 5mg vial and £120-£180 for a 10mg vial as of 2026. A listing priced dramatically below that, especially paired with no COA and no findable company registration, is one of the more reliable signs that what's being sold isn't what it claims to be.

### Watch for stock that's "always available"

Genuine sellers occasionally run out of a specific batch between manufacturing runs. A listing that's never once shown as out of stock, combined with no batch number changing over months, can indicate the seller isn't tracking real batch-specific inventory at all.

## Check the seller behind the listing

Search the company name on Companies House and look for a UK contact address. A listing hosted on a marketplace or a site with no findable company details behind it removes your ability to verify anything beyond the listing text itself, which is a meaningfully weaker position than buying from a registered UK business.

### What a genuine listing looks like

A listing like Enzo Meds shows a matching batch number between the product page and the COA, a stated UK company, and pricing consistent with the market range, which is the pattern worth comparing any tirzepatide listing against.

## Check the product description for copied text

Search a distinctive sentence or two from the product description in quotation marks on a general search engine. Text copied word-for-word from another seller's listing, or from a manufacturer's marketing material, suggests the description wasn't written specifically for that seller's own stock, which is worth weighing alongside the other checks.

### Watch for inconsistent product naming

A listing that switches between different names for the same product (tirzepatide, "GLP-1/GIP peptide," a vague brand name) within the same page can indicate the seller is deliberately vague about what's actually being sold, sometimes to avoid search platform restrictions rather than for any legitimate reason.

## Quick summary

Check the batch number is visible and matches the COA, confirm the COA names a method and lab, compare the price to the £70-£180 range typical for genuine UK stock, and verify the seller behind the listing is a real, findable UK company. A listing failing two or more of these is not worth ordering from.
