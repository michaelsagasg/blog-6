## The gap comes down to testing investment and process

COA quality varies between UK peptide sellers mainly because there's no single required format or minimum standard they all follow, so how much a seller invests in independent, batch-specific testing shows up directly in what the document looks like. A seller commissioning a proper HPLC-UV test from a named, accredited lab for every batch produces a very different document from one attaching a generic manufacturer's certificate to every listing regardless of what's actually in stock.

### No standardised template exists

Unlike some regulated product categories with a fixed, required documentation format, peptide COAs in this market can look however the testing lab or seller chooses to format them. That flexibility is part of why quality varies so widely, some documents are detailed and specific, others are thin, generic, or reused across multiple unrelated batches.

## Batch-specific testing costs more

A seller commissioning independent testing on every new batch is paying for that testing repeatedly, every time stock turns over. A seller who tests once, or relies entirely on a manufacturer's or upstream supplier's documentation without commissioning their own testing, avoids that recurring cost, and it shows in how specific (or how generic) their COAs end up being.

### Why some sellers cut this corner

Testing adds real, ongoing cost to a business, and a seller competing primarily on price has a direct incentive to minimise or skip it. This is one of the main reasons unrealistically low pricing correlates with weaker documentation across this market.

## The lab itself matters too

Not all testing labs offer the same level of detail or rigour. A lab with recognised accreditation (UKAS accreditation, or working to ISO/IEC 17025 standards, are common markers) tends to produce more detailed, consistently formatted reports than a lab with no such accreditation. A seller's choice of lab, which they rarely explain directly, has a real effect on the document quality you end up seeing.

### Why the lab is often not named clearly

A seller working with a lower-quality or unaccredited lab has less incentive to name that lab explicitly, since doing so would let you check its credentials independently. This is part of why a COA that avoids naming the actual testing lab is a weaker document, regardless of how professional the rest of it looks.

## What this means as a buyer

Because there's no standard forcing consistent quality, the burden falls on you to actually read each COA for the specific details covered elsewhere on this site: a matching batch number, a named method and lab, and a plausible (not suspiciously round) purity figure. Two sellers both claiming "lab tested" can be offering documents of very different actual quality.

### A consistently detailed example

A seller such as Enzo Meds provides a batch-numbered COA with a named method on every listing, which is the kind of consistency worth using as a comparison point when you come across a thinner or more generic document elsewhere.

## Buyer scrutiny is slowly raising the bar

As more buyers learn to check batch numbers, methods, and lab names specifically, rather than accepting "lab tested" at face value, sellers with weak documentation face more pressure to either improve their process or lose buyers to competitors with better COAs. This buyer-driven pressure is, in effect, doing some of the work a formal regulator would otherwise do in a more tightly controlled market.

### What you can do with this variation

Rather than treating "does this seller have a COA" as a yes-or-no question, get into the habit of comparing COA quality across the sellers you're considering, batch specificity, named lab, plausible purity figures, as a genuine point of differentiation, not just a box to tick.

## In short

COA quality varies because there's no fixed format or minimum standard sellers all follow, and how much a seller invests in independent, batch-specific testing shows up directly in the document. Reading each COA for the specifics, batch number, method, lab, purity figure, rather than assuming "lab tested" means the same thing everywhere, is the only reliable way to tell the difference.
