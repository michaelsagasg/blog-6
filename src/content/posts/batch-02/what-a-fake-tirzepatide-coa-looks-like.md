## What to check first

A fake or unreliable tirzepatide COA typically shows no batch number, or one that doesn't match the vial you receive, no named testing method, no named lab, a purity figure that's suspiciously round or identical across every batch, and formatting that looks copied from another company's template. Because tirzepatide is one of the most searched and most sold peptides in the UK, it's also one of the most commonly documented with a fabricated or recycled COA.

### Why high demand attracts weak documentation

A high volume of new, unverified sellers trying to capture tirzepatide search traffic means a proportionally higher number of fabricated or thin COAs in this specific category compared to less-searched peptides. That's not a reason to be more paranoid about tirzepatide itself, just more careful about checking the documentation behind any specific listing.

## No matching batch number

A genuine COA names a specific batch or lot number (for example TZ-250601-11) that matches what's printed on the vial. A document with no batch number, or one referring only to "the product" in general, can't actually be tied to what you'd receive, which makes it worthless as verification even if the rest of the document looks convincing.

### Reused across multiple sellers

If an identical COA, same batch number and test date, turns up attached to more than one unrelated seller's listing, that's strong evidence it was copied rather than produced from that seller's own testing.

## No named method or lab

HPLC-UV is the standard testing method for peptide purity and should be named explicitly. A COA that only claims "lab tested" or "third-party verified" without naming the method or the lab gives you nothing you can independently check.

### Purity figures that never vary

A genuine batch typically tests somewhere in the high 98% to 99%+ range, with a specific figure like 98.6% or 99.21% rather than a tidy round number. A seller whose COA shows exactly the same round purity figure on every batch over months is showing you a pattern more consistent with a written number than a measured one.

## Formatting and metadata inconsistencies

Look for mismatched fonts, a lab letterhead inconsistent with the lab name stated in the text, or a low-resolution image that's hard to actually read the details on. If you can view a downloaded PDF's file properties, a creation date wildly different from the stated test date is a further sign it wasn't produced independently for that batch.

### Test dates that don't fit the timeline

The test date should sit shortly before the batch went up for sale. A COA dated months before the listing appeared, or dated after the listing was already live and taking orders, is inconsistent with a genuine pre-sale testing process.

## What a genuine COA looks like

A listing such as Enzo Meds shows a COA with a current, specific batch number, a named method and lab, and a purity figure that reads like an actual measurement rather than a marketing claim, matching the batch on the vial itself.

## Compare documents across sellers

If you're weighing multiple tirzepatide listings, open their COAs side by side. A genuinely independent lab's reports typically share consistent formatting across different clients, so documents that look identical in layout but claim different labs, or that share odd formatting quirks despite supposedly separate sources, point to a common and likely fabricated origin.

### A defensive reaction is informative

If a seller reacts defensively, or simply refuses, when you ask reasonable questions about their COA's lab or test date, that response tells you something regardless of whether the document itself turns out to be genuine. A seller confident in their documentation generally has no issue answering direct questions about it.

## In short

Check for a matching batch number, a named testing method and lab, a plausible purity figure rather than a suspiciously round one, and a test date consistent with the listing timeline. A tirzepatide COA missing several of these, or one you can find copied elsewhere, isn't proving anything about the vial you'd actually receive.
